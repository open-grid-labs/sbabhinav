Mehendi and haldi are often where the best photos of a wedding come from. Nobody is posing, everyone is laughing, there's colour everywhere and the family is completely itself. They're also easy to under-plan, because they feel informal. Here are our tips for getting beautiful mehendi and haldi photos, whether the function is in a family courtyard in Mandi or on a resort lawn in Manali.

## Haldi photography tips

### Pick the right spot and time

- **Open shade or soft daylight is ideal.** A courtyard, a veranda or a lawn under trees gives bright, even light on faces and makes the yellow of the turmeric glow.
- **Avoid harsh midday sun** in open areas, which causes squinting and strong shadows. Mid-morning or late afternoon is best.
- **Avoid dark indoor rooms** if possible. If the haldi has to be indoors, choose the room with the biggest window.

### Plan the backdrop

A simple backdrop of marigolds, a painted wall, a wooden door or the mountains themselves looks far better in photos than a cluttered corner. Move chairs, buckets and plastic bags out of the frame before you start.

### Decide on the order

Know who applies haldi first: usually the mother, grandmothers, and in many Himachali families the mama and maami. Tell your photographer, so they're in position for those first moments rather than catching them from behind a crowd.

### Wear something you don't mind ruining

Haldi stains. Pick a simple outfit in yellow, white, pastel or a soft floral. Avoid heavy jewellery that you'll worry about. Flowers make a lovely, washable alternative.

### Let it get messy

The best haldi photos come when the family stops being careful. Turmeric fights, water thrown, cousins ambushing each other: that's the energy you want to remember. We keep our cameras protected and stay close.

## Mehendi photography tips

### Light for the henna

Mehendi designs need soft, directional light to show the detail. Sit the bride near a window or in open shade, never under a single overhead bulb.

### Give the mehendi artist space, and give us time

The bride's mehendi takes hours. Ask your photographer to come for the key moments: the first strokes, the finished hands, the groom's name hidden in the design and the family dancing around. We don't need to photograph every minute.

### Plan detail shots

- Close-ups of the finished design on hands and feet
- The bride's hands with her rings or bangles
- The cone in the artist's hand
- Little details like the dupatta, flowers or the decor

### Keep the decor photogenic

Low seating, floor cushions, warm string lights, marigolds and pastel drapes look beautiful in photos. Bright, cold-coloured LED lights and busy patterned backdrops don't.

### Plan the music and dancing

Mehendi evenings often turn into a dance party. In Himachal, that often means a Nati, the circle dance where everyone joins hands. Let your photographer know when the dancing will start so they can be ready with the right lenses and lighting.

## Combining mehendi and haldi

Many couples now hold haldi in the morning and mehendi in the afternoon or evening on the same day. That works well for photos if:

- There's enough time between them for the bride to wash and change (at least two hours)
- The two functions are set up in different spots, so the photos don't look the same
- The photographer is booked for the full day

## Questions to ask your photographer

- Do you cover mehendi and haldi as a separate function, or as part of the wedding day?
- How do you protect your equipment at haldi?
- Will you capture the family as well as the bride and groom?
- Do you shoot short reels of mehendi and haldi?
